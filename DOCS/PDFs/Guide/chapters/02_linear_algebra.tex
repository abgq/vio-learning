\chapter{Vectors, Matrices and Linear Maps}
\label{ch:linalg}

\begin{objectives}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Build linear algebra from scratch: vectors, vector spaces, bases, coordinates, inner products and the
        cross product.
  \item Understand a matrix as a linear map, matrix multiplication as composition, and the transpose, inverse,
        rank and determinant.
  \item Master the cross-product matrix $\sx{a}$ and the identities used in every rotation derivation.
  \item Understand orthogonal matrices, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and prove that every 3D rotation has an axis.
  \item Understand symmetric and positive-definite matrices, quadratic forms and their ellipsoids, the language in
        which uncertainty is expressed.
\end{itemize}
\end{objectives}

Linear algebra is the grammar of state estimation. States are vectors, rotations and covariances are matrices,
sensor models are linearized into matrices, and every filter step is a handful of matrix products and solutions
of linear systems. This chapter develops the subject from first principles with the geometric meaning of each
concept in view. \Cref{ch:numlinalg} then turns to the numerical algorithms that make these operations reliable
on a computer.

\section{Vectors}

\subsection{Arrows and lists of numbers}

A \term{vector} is first of all a geometric object: an arrow with a length and a direction, such as a
displacement, a velocity or the direction of gravity. Two arrows with the same length and direction are the
same vector, wherever they are drawn. Vectors can be \emph{added} (place them head to tail; the sum goes from the
first tail to the last head) and \emph{scaled} (stretch by a real factor; a negative factor reverses the
direction).

To compute, we pick three mutually perpendicular unit arrows $e_1, e_2, e_3$, the \emph{axes}, and write every
vector as a combination $v = v_1e_1 + v_2e_2 + v_3e_3$. The three numbers $(v_1, v_2, v_3)$ are the
\term{coordinates} of $v$, and we stack them into a \emph{column}
\begin{equation}
  v = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} \in \R^3 .
\end{equation}
Addition and scaling act coordinate by coordinate: $(u + v)_i = u_i + v_i$ and $(\alpha v)_i = \alpha v_i$.
More generally $\R^n$ is the set of columns of $n$ real numbers. A state vector of a navigation filter, such as
the 15 numbers of an IMU error state, lives in $\R^{15}$, even though it cannot be drawn.

\begin{keyidea}[a vector is not its coordinates]
The same physical arrow has different coordinates in different sets of axes. The velocity of a drone is one
arrow, but its coordinates in the world frame and in the body frame differ. Throughout these notes a subscript
names the frame: $v_w$ and $v_b$ are the coordinates of the same vector in the world and body frames.
Forgetting which frame a coordinate vector refers to is the most common bug in navigation software.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Vector spaces}

Many objects besides arrows can be added and scaled: polynomials, functions, matrices. The abstraction that
captures all of them is the vector space.

\begin{definition}[Vector space]
A \term{vector space} over $\R$ is a set $V$ with an addition $u + v$ and a scalar multiplication $\alpha v$
($\alpha \in \R$) such that, for all $u, v, w \in V$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \R$:
$u + v = v + u$; $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$; there is a zero vector with $v + 0 = v$; every $v$ has a negative
$-v$ with $v + (-v) = 0$; $\alpha(u + v) = \alpha u + \alpha v$; $(\alpha + \beta)v = \alpha v + \beta v$;
$\alpha(\beta v) = (\alpha\beta)v$; and $1v = v$.
\end{definition}

Examples: $\R^n$; the set of all $m\times n$ matrices; the set of polynomials of degree at most $d$; and, of great
importance later, the set of $3\times3$ \emph{skew-symmetric} matrices ($S\T = -S$), which is closed under addition
and scaling and will turn out to be the ``tangent space'' of the rotation group (\cref{ch:lie}). A non-example:
the set of unit vectors in $\R^3$ (the sum of two unit vectors is usually not a unit vector). Likewise, the set of
rotation matrices is \emph{not} a vector space, which is the root of most of the difficulties of
\cref{ch:rotations,ch:lie}.

A \term{subspace} is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space under the same operations; it
suffices to check that it contains $0$ and is closed under addition and scaling. Lines and planes through the
origin are the subspaces of $\R^3$; a line that misses the origin is not.

\subsection{Linear combinations, span and independence}

\begin{definition}
A \term{linear combination} of $v_1, \dots, v_k$ is a vector $\alpha_1v_1 + \dots + \alpha_kv_k$. The
\term{span} of the $v_i$ is the set of all their linear combinations; it is a subspace. The vectors are
\term{linearly independent} if the only combination that gives zero is the trivial one:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_1v_1 + \dots + \alpha_kv_k = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_1 = \dots = \alpha_k = 0 .
\end{equation}
Otherwise they are \emph{linearly dependent}: one of them is a combination of the others.
\end{definition}

In $\R^3$: two vectors are dependent exactly when they are parallel; three vectors are dependent exactly when
they lie in a common plane through the origin. Testing independence means solving a homogeneous linear system,
which \cref{sec:linsys} shows how to do.

\subsection{Basis, dimension and coordinates}

\begin{definition}[Basis]
A \term{basis} of a vector space $V$ is a linearly independent set of vectors that spans $V$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Dimension]
All bases of a vector space have the same number of elements, called its \term{dimension}.
\end{theorem}

We omit the proof, which is a careful counting argument (a set of $k$ independent vectors cannot lie in the span
of fewer than $k$ vectors). $\R^n$ has dimension $n$, the $m\times n$ matrices have dimension $mn$, and the $3\times3$
skew-symmetric matrices have dimension three (three free entries), the same as the number of degrees of freedom
of a rotation.

\begin{proposition}[Coordinates are unique]
If $b_1, \dots, b_n$ is a basis, every $v \in V$ can be written as $v = \sum_i c_ib_i$ in exactly one way. The
numbers $c_i$ are the coordinates of $v$ in that basis.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Existence holds because the basis spans $V$. If also $v = \sum_i c_i'b_i$, subtracting gives
$\sum_i(c_i - c_i')b_i = 0$, and independence forces $c_i = c_i'$.
\end{proof}

Changing the basis changes the coordinates of every vector but not the vectors. \Cref{sec:orthogonal-matrices}
computes how coordinates change when one orthonormal basis is replaced by another, which is exactly what a
rotation matrix between two frames does.

\section{Inner products, lengths and angles}

\subsection{The dot product}

\begin{definition}[Dot product and norm]
The \term{dot product} (inner product) of $a, b \in \R^n$ is $a\T b = a\cdot b = \sum_i a_ib_i$, and the
(Euclidean) \term{norm} or length is $\|a\| = \sqrt{a\T a}$. A \emph{unit vector} has norm one; $a/\|a\|$
\emph{normalizes} a non-zero $a$.
\end{definition}

The dot product is symmetric ($a\T b = b\T a$), linear in each argument, and positive ($a\T a > 0$ for $a\ne0$).
Its geometric meaning comes from the law of cosines.

\begin{proposition}[Geometric dot product]
If $\theta$ is the angle between non-zero $a$ and $b$, then $a\T b = \|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The triangle with sides $a$, $b$ and $a - b$ satisfies the law of cosines
$\|a - b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$. Expanding the left side with bilinearity gives
$\|a\|^2 - 2a\T b + \|b\|^2$. Comparing the two expressions proves the claim.
\end{proof}

Vectors are \term{orthogonal} ($a \perp b$) when $a\T b = 0$. For orthogonal vectors the Pythagorean theorem
$\|a + b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2$ follows by expanding the left side.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz and triangle inequalities]
\index{Cauchy--Schwarz inequality}
For all $a, b \in \R^n$, $|a\T b| \le \|a\|\|b\|$ and $\|a + b\| \le \|a\| + \|b\|$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $b = 0$ both are trivial. Otherwise, for every real $t$, $0 \le \|a - tb\|^2 = \|a\|^2 - 2t\,a\T b + t^2\|b\|^2$.
A quadratic in $t$ that is never negative has a non-positive discriminant: $4(a\T b)^2 - 4\|a\|^2\|b\|^2 \le 0$,
which is the first inequality. Then
$\|a + b\|^2 = \|a\|^2 + 2a\T b + \|b\|^2 \le \|a\|^2 + 2\|a\|\|b\| + \|b\|^2 = (\|a\| + \|b\|)^2$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Orthonormal bases and projections}

A basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is \term{orthonormal} if $e_i\T e_j = 1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. In an orthonormal
basis, coordinates are simply dot products: dotting $v = \sum_j c_je_j$ with $e_i$ leaves
\begin{equation}
  c_i = e_i\T v .
  \label{eq:onb-coords}
\end{equation}
This is why navigation always uses orthonormal (Cartesian) axes: finding coordinates requires no equation solving.

\begin{proposition}[Projection onto a direction]
\label{prop:projection-line}
For $b \neq 0$, the point of the line $\{tb\}$ closest to $a$ is $\mathrm{proj}_b a = \frac{a\T b}{b\T b}b$, and the
remainder $a - \mathrm{proj}_b a$ is orthogonal to $b$ (\cref{fig:projection}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$f(t) = \|a - tb\|^2 = \|a\|^2 - 2t\,a\T b + t^2\|b\|^2$ is a parabola minimized where $f'(t) = 0$, i.e.\
$t = a\T b/b\T b$. Then $b\T(a - tb) = a\T b - t\,b\T b = 0$.
\end{proof}

Every vector therefore splits into a component along $b$ and a component perpendicular to it. This decomposition
is the heart of Rodrigues' rotation formula (\cref{ch:rotations}): a rotation leaves the component along its axis
alone and turns the perpendicular component.

\paragraph{Gram--Schmidt.}\index{Gram--Schmidt}
Any basis $a_1, \dots, a_n$ can be turned into an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_n$ spanning the same nested
subspaces, by subtracting from each vector its projections onto the previous ones and normalizing:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde q_k = a_k - \sum_{j<k}(q_j\T a_k)\,q_j, \qquad q_k = \tilde q_k/\|\tilde q_k\| .
\end{equation}
By construction $\tilde q_k$ is orthogonal to $q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}$, and it cannot be zero because $a_k$ is not in
the span of the previous vectors. Recording the coefficients $q_j\T a_k$ gives the QR factorization of
\cref{ch:numlinalg}.

\section{The cross product}
\label{sec:skew}

\subsection{Definition and geometry}

\begin{definition}[Cross product]
For $a, b \in \R^3$,
\begin{equation}
  a \times b = \begin{bmatrix} a_2b_3 - a_3b_2 \\ a_3b_1 - a_1b_3 \\ a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\index{cross product}
A convenient mnemonic is the formal determinant with rows $(e_1, e_2, e_3)$, $(a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and
$(b_1, b_2, b_3)$. The cross product is \emph{bilinear}, \emph{anti-commutative} ($a \times b = -b \times a$, so
$a \times a = 0$), and orthogonal to both factors: $a\T(a \times b) = 0$, which can be verified by expanding.

\begin{proposition}[Length of the cross product]
$\|a\times b\|^2 = \|a\|^2\|b\|^2 - (a\T b)^2$ (Lagrange's identity), hence
$\|a \times b\| = \|a\|\|b\|\sin\theta$, the area of the parallelogram spanned by $a$ and $b$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expand both sides in coordinates; all terms match. Then substitute $a\T b = \|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$ and use
$1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta$.
\end{proof}

The direction of $a \times b$ follows the \term{right-hand rule}: with the fingers of the right hand curling from
$a$ to $b$, the thumb points along $a \times b$. In particular $e_1 \times e_2 = e_3$, $e_2 \times e_3 = e_1$ and
$e_3 \times e_1 = e_2$. A coordinate system obeying these relations is \emph{right-handed}; all frames in these
notes are.

The \emph{scalar triple product} $a\T(b \times c)$ is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $a$, $b$
and $c$; it equals the determinant of the matrix with columns $a, b, c$ (\cref{sec:determinants}), is unchanged by
cyclic permutations, and changes sign when two vectors are swapped. The \emph{vector triple product} obeys the
``BAC--CAB'' rule
\begin{equation}
  a \times (b \times c) = b\,(a\T c) - c\,(a\T b),
  \label{eq:bac-cab}
\end{equation}
which can be checked in coordinates (it suffices to check basis vectors, by linearity).

\subsection{The cross-product matrix}

Because $a \times b$ is linear in $b$, it is a matrix-vector product (\cref{sec:matrices}).

\begin{definition}[Skew-symmetric matrix]
For $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)\T$, the \term{cross-product matrix} (or skew-symmetric matrix) of $a$ is
\begin{equation}
  \sx{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a_3 & a_2 \\ a_3 & 0 & -a_1 \\ -a_2 & a_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \sx{a}\, b = a \times b \quad \forall\, b \in \R^3 .
  \label{eq:skew}
\end{equation}
It is skew-symmetric: $\sx{a}\T = -\sx{a}$. Conversely, every $3\times3$ skew-symmetric matrix $S$ equals $\sx{s}$ for
the unique vector $s = (S_{32}, S_{13}, S_{21})\T$; we write $s = S\veeop$ for this inverse operation, the
\emph{vee} map. In Lie theory $\sx{\cdot}$ is also written $(\cdot)\hatop$ and called the \emph{hat} map.
\end{definition}

The following identities are used constantly in derivations; learn to recognize them on sight.

\begin{proposition}[Cross-product identities]
\label{prop:skew-identities}
For $a, b, c \in \R^3$ and a rotation matrix $R$ (\cref{sec:orthogonal-matrices}):
\begin{align}
  \sx{a}\, b &= -\sx{b}\, a, \label{eq:skew-anticomm}\\
  \sx{a}\, a &= 0, \label{eq:skew-self}\\
  \sx{a}^2 &= a a\T - \|a\|^2 I, \label{eq:skew-sq}\\
  \sx{a}^3 &= -\|a\|^2 \sx{a}, \label{eq:skew-cube}\\
  \sx{a}\sx{b} &= b a\T - (a\T b)\, I, \label{eq:skew-prod}\\
  \sx{a \times b} &= \sx{a}\sx{b} - \sx{b}\sx{a} = b a\T - a b\T, \label{eq:skew-commutator}\\
  \sx{R a} &= R \sx{a} R\T, \label{eq:skew-rot}\\
  R\,(a \times b) &= (Ra) \times (Rb). \label{eq:cross-rot}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\eqref{eq:skew-anticomm} and \eqref{eq:skew-self} restate anti-commutativity. For \eqref{eq:skew-prod}, apply both sides to
an arbitrary $c$ and use \eqref{eq:bac-cab}: $\sx{a}\sx{b}c = a\times(b\times c) = b\,a\T c - c\,(a\T b)$. Setting $b = a$ gives
\eqref{eq:skew-sq}, and multiplying \eqref{eq:skew-sq} on the left by $\sx{a}$ and using \eqref{eq:skew-self} gives
\eqref{eq:skew-cube}. Subtracting \eqref{eq:skew-prod} from its version with $a$ and $b$ swapped gives the second equality
of \eqref{eq:skew-commutator}; applying $ba\T - ab\T$ to $c$ and using \eqref{eq:bac-cab} once more shows it equals
$\sx{a\times b}c$. For \eqref{eq:cross-rot}, the cross product is defined by lengths, angles and orientation, all
preserved by a rotation (\cref{sec:orthogonal-matrices}). Finally \eqref{eq:skew-rot} follows from \eqref{eq:cross-rot}:
$\sx{Ra}\,c = (Ra)\times(RR\T c) = R\,(a \times R\T c) = R\sx{a}R\T c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{keyidea}[moving a vector out of a cross product]
Identity \eqref{eq:skew-anticomm} is how unknowns are isolated in Jacobian derivations. A small rotation error
$\delta\theta$ typically appears as $\sx{\delta\theta}\,v$; rewriting it as $-\sx{v}\,\delta\theta$ makes it a matrix
times the unknown, and the matrix $-\sx{v}$ is the Jacobian.
\end{keyidea}

Identity \eqref{eq:skew-cube} is what makes closed-form rotation formulas possible: every power of $\sx{a}$ reduces to
a multiple of $\sx{a}$ or $\sx{a}^2$, so infinite series in $\sx{a}$ collapse to two terms (\cref{ch:rotations}).

\section{Matrices as linear maps}
\label{sec:matrices}

\subsection{Matrices and the matrix-vector product}

An $m\times n$ \term{matrix} $A$ is a rectangular array of numbers $A_{ij}$ with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Its product
with $x \in \R^n$ is the vector $Ax \in \R^m$ with entries $(Ax)_i = \sum_j A_{ij}x_j$. There are two ways to read
this product, and both are indispensable:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  Ax = \begin{bmatrix} r_1\T x \\ \vdots \\ r_m\T x \end{bmatrix}
  \quad\text{(dot products with the rows $r_i\T$)},
  \\[2pt]
  Ax = x_1a_1 + x_2a_2 + \dots + x_na_n
  \quad\text{(combination of the columns $a_j$)}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The column view says that the possible outputs $Ax$ are exactly the linear combinations of the columns of $A$.

\subsection{Linear maps}

\begin{definition}[Linear map]
A function $f: \R^n \to \R^m$ is \term{linear} if $f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v)$ and $f(\alpha u) = \alpha f(u)$ for all
vectors $u, v$ and scalars $\alpha$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Linear maps are matrices]
Every linear map $f: \R^n\to\R^m$ is multiplication by a unique $m\times n$ matrix, whose $j$-th column is $f(e_j)$, the
image of the $j$-th basis vector.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $x = \sum_j x_je_j$. By linearity $f(x) = \sum_jx_jf(e_j)$, which is the column view of $Ax$ with columns
$a_j = f(e_j)$. Uniqueness: two matrices that agree on all basis vectors have the same columns.
\end{proof}

This theorem turns geometric questions into matrix questions: to find the matrix of a geometric operation, ask
where it sends each basis vector.

\begin{example}[Rotation in the plane]
\label{ex:rot2d}
Rotating the plane counter-clockwise by $\theta$ sends $e_1 = (1, 0)$ to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and
$e_2 = (0, 1)$ to $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$. Rotation is linear (it preserves parallelograms), so its matrix is
\begin{equation}
  R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\subsection{Matrix multiplication is composition}

If $B$ is $m\times n$ and $A$ is $p\times m$, applying $B$ and then $A$ is again linear, and its matrix is the
\term{matrix product} $AB$: $(AB)x = A(Bx)$. Its $j$-th column is $A$ applied to the $j$-th column of $B$, and its
entries are
\begin{equation}
  (AB)_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}.
\end{equation}
Because composition of functions is associative, so is matrix multiplication: $(AB)C = A(BC)$. It is \emph{not}
commutative: rotating and then shearing is not the same as shearing and then rotating. For rotations in three
dimensions non-commutativity is unavoidable and is the defining difficulty of attitude estimation. The
\term{identity matrix} $I$, with ones on the diagonal, leaves every vector unchanged: $AI = IA = A$.

\paragraph{Reading a product of transformations.} In $ABx$, the transformation applied first is the one closest to
$x$, here $B$. With frame-annotated rotations (\cref{ch:rotations}), $R_{wb}R_{bc}x_c$ first expresses the camera
vector $x_c$ in the body frame and then in the world frame.

\subsection{Transpose and special matrices}

The \term{transpose} $A\T$ swaps rows and columns: $(A\T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$. The dot product is a matrix product,
$a\T b$, and transposition reverses products:
\begin{equation}
  (AB)\T = B\T A\T,
\end{equation}
because $((AB)\T)_{ki} = (AB)_{ik} = \sum_jA_{ij}B_{jk} = \sum_j(B\T)_{kj}(A\T)_{ji}$. A matrix is \emph{symmetric} if
$A\T = A$ and \emph{skew-symmetric} if $A\T = -A$; every square matrix splits uniquely as
$A = \tfrac12(A + A\T) + \tfrac12(A - A\T)$, a symmetric plus a skew-symmetric part. Covariance matrices are symmetric;
cross-product matrices are skew-symmetric.

Other matrices with names: \emph{diagonal} matrices (non-zero entries only on the diagonal), \emph{upper} and
\emph{lower triangular} matrices (zeros below or above the diagonal), and the \term{outer product} $ab\T$ of two
vectors, an $m\times n$ matrix with entries $a_ib_j$ whose columns are all multiples of $a$.

\paragraph{Block matrices.} Matrices can be partitioned into blocks, and blocks multiply like entries as long as
their sizes are compatible:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} E & F \\ G & H \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} AE + BG & AF + BH \\ CE + DG & CF + DH \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Filters are written almost entirely in block form: the IMU state and the clones, position and attitude, poses and
landmarks.

\section{Linear systems, inverses and rank}
\label{sec:linsys}

\subsection{Solving linear systems}

Given $A$ and $b$, find $x$ with $Ax = b$. In the column view: find the combination of the columns of $A$ that
produces $b$. Three things can happen: exactly one solution, none ($b$ is not a combination of the columns), or
infinitely many (some combination of the columns gives zero, and it can be added to any solution).

\term{Gaussian elimination} solves the system by \emph{row operations} that do not change the solution set:
swapping two equations, scaling an equation by a non-zero number, and subtracting a multiple of one equation from
another. They are used to create zeros below the diagonal (the \emph{pivots}) column by column, after which the
equations are solved from the bottom up by \emph{back substitution}.

\begin{example}[Elimination]
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
  2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 &= 5 \\ 4x_1 + 3x_2 + 3x_3 &= 13 \\ 2x_1 + 3x_2 + 4x_3 &= 13
\end{aligned}
\quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{(2)}-2\text{(1)},\ \text{(3)}-\text{(1)}\ }\quad
\begin{aligned}
  2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 &= 5 \\ x_2 + x_3 &= 3 \\ 2x_2 + 3x_3 &= 8
\end{aligned}
\quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{(3)}-2\text{(2)}\ }\quad
\begin{aligned}
  2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 &= 5 \\ x_2 + x_3 &= 3 \\ x_3 &= 2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Back substitution gives $x_3 = 2$, $x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 = 1$. The multipliers $2, 1, 2$ used along the way are the entries
of the lower-triangular factor $L$ of an $LU$ factorization $A = LU$ (\cref{ch:numlinalg}).
\end{example}

\subsection{The inverse}

A square matrix $A$ is \term{invertible} if there is a matrix $A\inv$ with $AA\inv = A\inv A = I$. Then $Ax = b$ has the
unique solution $x = A\inv b$. Useful facts, each verified by multiplying out:
\begin{equation}
  (AB)\inv = B\inv A\inv, \qquad (A\T)\inv = (A\inv)\T, \qquad
  \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\inv = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For a square matrix the following are equivalent: $A$ is invertible; $Ax = 0$ only for $x = 0$; the columns are
independent; elimination produces $n$ non-zero pivots; $\det A \ne 0$ (\cref{sec:determinants}). In numerical work one
almost never computes $A\inv$ explicitly; \cref{ch:numlinalg} explains why and what to do instead.

\subsection{Range, nullspace and rank}

\begin{definition}
For $A \in \R^{m\times n}$: the \term{range} (column space) $\range(A) = \{Ax\} \subseteq \R^m$; the \term{nullspace}
$\nullsp(A) = \{x : Ax = 0\} \subseteq \R^n$; and the \term{rank} $\rank A = \dim\range(A)$.
\end{definition}

The rank is the number of independent columns, and also the number of pivots produced by elimination. A
surprising and useful fact is that row rank equals column rank: $\rank A = \rank A\T$.

\begin{theorem}[Rank--nullity]
\index{rank--nullity theorem}
For $A \in \R^{m\times n}$, $\dim\nullsp(A) + \rank A = n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Choose a basis $u_1, \dots, u_k$ of $\nullsp(A)$ and extend it with $w_1, \dots, w_r$ to a basis of $\R^n$, so $k + r = n$. The
vectors $Aw_1, \dots, Aw_r$ span $\range(A)$ (the $Au_i$ vanish), and they are independent: if $\sum c_iAw_i = 0$ then
$\sum c_iw_i \in \nullsp(A)$, which forces all $c_i = 0$ because the $u$'s and $w$'s together are independent. So
$\rank A = r = n - k$.
\end{proof}

Each dimension of the nullspace is a direction of $x$ that the map destroys. In estimation, the nullspace of a
measurement Jacobian $H$ is the set of state errors the measurement cannot see (\cref{ch:updates}), and the
nullspace of an observability matrix is the set of state directions no measurement history can determine
(\cref{ch:linsys,ch:observability}).

\subsection{The four fundamental subspaces}
\label{sec:subspaces}

\index{fundamental subspaces}
Each $A \in \R^{m\times n}$ of rank $r$ has four associated subspaces:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l l l@{}}
\toprule
Subspace & Definition & Lives in & Dimension \\
\midrule
column space (range) & $\range(A) = \{Ax\}$ & $\R^m$ & $r$ \\
nullspace & $\nullsp(A) = \{x : Ax = 0\}$ & $\R^n$ & $n - r$ \\
row space & $\range(A\T)$ & $\R^n$ & $r$ \\
left nullspace & $\nullsp(A\T) = \{y : y\T A = 0\}$ & $\R^m$ & $m - r$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental theorem of linear algebra]
\label{thm:fundamental}
$\nullsp(A) = \range(A\T)^\perp$ and $\nullsp(A\T) = \range(A)^\perp$, where $S^\perp$ denotes the set of vectors orthogonal
to every vector of $S$. Consequently every $y \in \R^m$ splits uniquely as $y = y_r + y_n$ with $y_r \in \range(A)$ and
$y_n \in \nullsp(A\T)$, $y_r \perp y_n$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$y \in \nullsp(A\T) \iff A\T y = 0 \iff x\T A\T y = 0\ \forall x \iff (Ax)\T y = 0\ \forall x \iff y \perp \range(A)$. The
first statement is the same argument applied to $A\T$. The decomposition follows by projecting $y$ onto $\range(A)$
(\cref{ch:numlinalg}).
\end{proof}

The \emph{left nullspace} deserves special attention because the MSCKF is built on it. Suppose a stacked measurement
depends on an unwanted variable $f$ through a tall matrix $H_f \in \R^{m\times3}$ of full column rank:
\begin{equation}
  r = H_x\,\delta x + H_f\,\delta f + n .
\end{equation}
Take any matrix $A$ whose $m - 3$ columns form an orthonormal basis of $\nullsp(H_f\T)$. Then $A\T H_f = 0$, and
multiplying through by $A\T$ gives $A\T r = A\T H_x\,\delta x + A\T n$: the unwanted variable has disappeared.
\Cref{ch:msckfupdate} proves that no information about $\delta x$ is lost.

\subsection{Determinants as signed volumes}

\centering

  \fill[primary!18, draw=primary, thick] (0,0) -- (3,0.6) -- (3.8,2.2) -- (0.8,1.6) -- cycle;

  \node[primary, font=\small] at (2.0,1.15) {area $= |ad - bc|$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by its columns; the sign is

\label{fig:det-area}
\end{figure}

A linear map sends the unit square (cube) to a parallelogram (parallelepiped). The \term{determinant} is the factor by
which it scales areas (volumes), with a sign that records whether it preserves or reverses orientation. For a
$2\times2$ matrix the parallelogram spanned by the columns $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ has signed area $ad - bc$
(\cref{fig:det-area}); for a $3\times3$ matrix with columns $a_1, a_2, a_3$ the signed volume is the triple product:
\begin{equation}
  \det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc, \qquad
  \det\begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \end{bmatrix} = a_1\T(a_2 \times a_3).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Definition and properties}

In general, the determinant is the unique function of the columns of a square matrix that is
\begin{enumerate}
  \item linear in each column separately (\emph{multilinear}),
  \item zero whenever two columns are equal (\emph{alternating}; equivalently, it changes sign when two columns are
        swapped), and
  \item normalized: $\det I = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
These three rules determine everything. Adding a multiple of one column to another does not change the determinant
(by linearity, the change is a determinant with two equal columns, which is zero); hence the same holds for rows, and
elimination reduces any determinant to that of a triangular matrix, which is the product of its diagonal entries.
Expanding along the first row gives the \emph{cofactor} (Laplace) formula
\begin{equation}
  \det A = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{1+j}A_{1j}\det M_{1j},
\end{equation}
where $M_{1j}$ deletes row 1 and column $j$. It is convenient for $3\times3$ matrices by hand and hopeless for large ones,
whose determinants are computed from an $LU$ or Cholesky factorization.

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the determinant]
For square $A$ and $B$ of the same size:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$ (volume scaling factors multiply under composition);
  \item $\det A\T = \det A$;
  \item $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \ne 0$, and then $\det A\inv = 1/\det A$;
  \item the determinant of a triangular or diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Property 3 has a clear geometric meaning: $\det A = 0$ means the map flattens space onto a lower-dimensional subspace,
destroying information, so it cannot be undone. The sign of the determinant distinguishes \emph{rotations} (which
preserve orientation, $\det = +1$) from \emph{reflections} (which reverse it, $\det = -1$); the next section makes
this precise.

\section{Orthogonal matrices and change of basis}
\label{sec:orthogonal-matrices}

\begin{definition}[Orthogonal matrix]
A square matrix $Q$ is \term{orthogonal} if $Q\T Q = I$, i.e.\ its columns are orthonormal. Then $Q\inv = Q\T$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal matrices preserve geometry]
If $Q$ is orthogonal then $(Qa)\T(Qb) = a\T b$ for all $a, b$: lengths and angles are preserved. Moreover
$\det Q = \pm1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$(Qa)\T(Qb) = a\T Q\T Qb = a\T b$. And $1 = \det I = \det(Q\T Q) = (\det Q)^2$.
\end{proof}

Orthogonal matrices with $\det Q = +1$ are \term{rotations}; those with $\det Q = -1$ are rotations combined with a
reflection, which turn right-handed frames into left-handed ones. The orthogonal $n\times n$ matrices form the
\emph{orthogonal group} $\mathrm O(n)$, and the rotations form the \emph{special orthogonal group} $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.
Products and inverses of rotations are rotations.

\paragraph{Change of orthonormal basis.} Let $e_1, e_2, e_3$ be the axes of a frame $W$ and $b_1, b_2, b_3$ those of a
frame $B$, and let $R$ be the matrix whose columns are the coordinates of $b_1, b_2, b_3$ in $W$. A vector with
coordinates $v_b$ in $B$ is $v = \sum_j(v_b)_jb_j$; its coordinates in $W$ are therefore
\begin{equation}
  v_w = \sum_j (v_b)_j\,(\text{coordinates of } b_j \text{ in } W) = R\,v_b,
  \qquad v_b = R\T v_w .
\end{equation}
The matrix $R$ is orthogonal because its columns are orthonormal, and it is a rotation when both frames are
right-handed. This is exactly the rotation matrix $R_{wb}$ of \cref{ch:rotations}: its columns are the body axes in world
coordinates, and it maps body coordinates to world coordinates.

\section{Eigenvalues and eigenvectors}

\subsection{Definitions}

\begin{definition}
A non-zero vector $v$ is an \term{eigenvector} of a square matrix $A$, with \term{eigenvalue} $\lambda$, if
$Av = \lambda v$: the map only stretches $v$, by the factor $\lambda$.
\end{definition}

Rewriting $Av = \lambda v$ as $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$ shows that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $A - \lambda I$ has a
non-trivial nullspace, i.e.\ when the \term{characteristic polynomial} vanishes:
\begin{equation}
  p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 .
\end{equation}
For a $2\times2$ matrix, $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - (\tr A)\lambda + \det A$, where $\tr A$ is the sum of the diagonal entries (the
\term{trace}). An $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, which may be complex even when $A$ is
real. Comparing the coefficients of $p(\lambda) = \prod_i(\lambda_i - \lambda)$ shows that
\begin{equation}
  \tr A = \sum_i\lambda_i, \qquad \det A = \prod_i\lambda_i .
  \label{eq:trace-det-eig}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Complex numbers in one paragraph}

A complex number $z = x + iy$ has a real part $x$ and an imaginary part $y$, with $i^2 = -1$. It can be pictured as the
point $(x, y)$ of the plane; its modulus is $|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and its argument is the angle of the point. Euler's
formula
\begin{equation}
  e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta
\end{equation}
follows from the power series of $e^x$, $\cos x$ and $\sin x$ by substituting $x = i\theta$. Multiplying a complex number by
$e^{i\theta}$ rotates the corresponding point by $\theta$: complex multiplication \emph{is} planar rotation. Quaternions
(\cref{ch:rotations}) generalize this idea to three dimensions.

\begin{example}[Eigenvalues of a planar rotation]
\label{ex:rot2d-eig}
For $R(\theta)$ of \cref{ex:rot2d}, $\tr R = 2\cos\theta$ and $\det R = 1$, so $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta\,\lambda + 1$ with
roots $\lambda = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}$. Unless $\theta$ is a multiple of $\pi$, no real direction is merely
stretched, as expected of a rotation.
\end{example}

\subsection{Diagonalization}

If $A \in \R^{n\times n}$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$, collect them in $V = [v_1 \cdots v_n]$ and the
eigenvalues in $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. The relations $Av_i = \lambda_iv_i$ read $AV = V\Lambda$, so
\begin{equation}
  A = V\Lambda V\inv .
\end{equation}
In the basis of eigenvectors, $A$ acts by independent scalings. Powers and polynomials become trivial,
$A^k = V\Lambda^kV\inv$, and so do the power series used to define the matrix exponential (\cref{ch:numlinalg}). Eigenvectors
of distinct eigenvalues are always independent, so a matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. Not every
matrix is: the shear $\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ has the double eigenvalue $1$ but only one eigenvector
direction.

\subsection{Every 3D rotation has an axis}

\begin{theorem}[Euler's rotation theorem]
\label{thm:euler-axis}
\index{Euler's rotation theorem}
Every $R \in \SO$ has $1$ as an eigenvalue. Its eigenvalues are $1, e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}$ for some $\theta \in [0, \pi]$,
and $\tr R = 1 + 2\cos\theta$. The real eigenvector $u$ of eigenvalue 1 is fixed by $R$: it is the \emph{axis} of the
rotation, and $R$ rotates the plane orthogonal to $u$ by the angle $\theta$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Using $R\T R = I$ and $\det R = 1$:
$\det(R - I) = \det\big(R(I - R\T)\big) = \det R\,\det\big((I - R)\T\big) = \det(I - R) = (-1)^3\det(R - I)$, so $\det(R - I) = 0$ and
$1$ is an eigenvalue. Every eigenvalue has modulus one, because $R$ preserves lengths (this holds for complex eigenvectors as
well). The characteristic polynomial is real and of degree three, so non-real eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs; with
one eigenvalue equal to 1 and the product of all three equal to $\det R = 1$, the other two are $e^{\pm i\theta}$, or both
$+1$ or both $-1$. The trace formula is \eqref{eq:trace-det-eig}. Finally, $R$ maps the plane orthogonal to $u$ to itself
(it preserves orthogonality to the fixed vector $u$), and it acts there as a planar rotation with eigenvalues
$e^{\pm i\theta}$, i.e.\ by the angle $\theta$.
\end{proof}

This theorem is the reason a rotation can always be described by an axis and an angle, the representation from which
all of rotation calculus is built (\cref{ch:rotations,ch:lie}).

\section{Symmetric matrices and quadratic forms}

Covariance matrices, information matrices and the normal equations of least squares are all symmetric. Symmetric
matrices have the most beautiful eigenstructure of all.

\begin{theorem}[Spectral theorem]
\label{thm:spectral}
\index{spectral theorem}
A real symmetric matrix $A$ has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors:
$A = U\Lambda U\T$ with $U$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ real diagonal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Real eigenvalues.} Let $Av = \lambda v$ with $v$ possibly complex and $\bar v$ its complex conjugate. The number
$s = \bar v\T Av$ equals $\lambda\,\bar v\T v$. Its complex conjugate is $v\T A\bar v$ (because $A$ is real), and a $1\times1$ matrix equals its
own transpose, so $\bar s = \bar v\T A\T v = \bar v\T Av = s$: $s$ is real. Since $\bar v\T v = \sum_i|v_i|^2 > 0$,
$\lambda = s/\bar v\T v$ is real too.
\emph{Orthogonality.} If $Av = \lambda v$ and $Aw = \mu w$ with $\lambda \ne \mu$, then
$\lambda\,w\T v = w\T Av = (Aw)\T v = \mu\,w\T v$, so $w\T v = 0$.
\emph{Completeness} (sketch, by induction on $n$). Take a unit eigenvector $u_1$. The subspace $u_1^\perp$ is mapped into itself
by $A$: if $x \perp u_1$ then $u_1\T Ax = (Au_1)\T x = \lambda_1u_1\T x = 0$. Restricted to this $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace, $A$ is
again symmetric and, by induction, has an orthonormal eigenbasis, which together with $u_1$ is an orthonormal eigenbasis
of $\R^n$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Quadratic forms and definiteness}

A \term{quadratic form} is a function $x \mapsto x\T Ax$ with $A$ symmetric. Substituting $x = Uy$, with $U$ from the
spectral theorem, decouples it into independent squares:
\begin{equation}
  x\T Ax = y\T\Lambda y = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2 .
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Definiteness]
A symmetric matrix $A$ is \term{positive definite} (PD, $A \succ 0$) if $x\T Ax > 0$ for every $x \ne 0$, and
\term{positive semi-definite} (PSD, $A \succeq 0$) if $x\T Ax \ge 0$ for every $x$. We write $A \succeq B$ (the
\emph{Loewner order}) when $A - B \succeq 0$.
\end{definition}

By the decoupled form, $A \succ 0$ if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive, and $A \succeq 0$ if and only if they are
non-negative. Other tests: all pivots of elimination are positive; the Cholesky factorization exists
(\cref{ch:numlinalg}); or all leading principal minors are positive (Sylvester's criterion).

\centering

\paragraph{Ellipsoids.} For $P \succ 0$ the set $\{x : x\T P\inv x \le 1\}$ is an ellipsoid with principal axes along the
eigenvectors of $P$ and semi-axis lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ (\cref{fig:ellipse}): in the rotated coordinates $y = U\T x$ it reads
$\sum_iy_i^2/\lambda_i \le 1$. When $P$ is a covariance matrix this is the uncertainty ellipsoid of \cref{ch:prob}, and the
eigen-decomposition of $P$ tells in which directions the estimate is least certain.

\begin{proposition}[Properties of PSD matrices]
\label{prop:psd}
Let $P, Q \succeq 0$ and let $A$ be any matrix of compatible size. Then
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $A P A\T \succeq 0$ (a \emph{congruence} preserves semi-definiteness);
  \item $P + Q \succeq 0$, and $P + Q \succ 0$ if either is PD;
  \item $P_{ii} \ge 0$ and $|P_{ij}| \le \sqrt{P_{ii} P_{jj}}$;
  \item every principal submatrix of $P$ is PSD.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(1) $x\T A P A\T x = (A\T x)\T P (A\T x) \ge 0$. (2) is immediate. (4) follows from (1) with $A$ a selection matrix that picks some
coordinates; (3) is (4) applied to $1\times1$ and $2\times2$ principal submatrices, whose determinants $P_{ii}P_{jj} - P_{ij}^2$ are
non-negative.
\end{proof}

Property 1 explains why the covariance updates $\Phi P\Phi\T + Q$ and the Joseph form (\cref{ch:kf}) preserve positive
semi-definiteness exactly in exact arithmetic: they are sums of congruences of PSD matrices. The simpler update
$(I - KH)P$ is not a congruence, which is why it can lose semi-definiteness under rounding.

\begin{proposition}[Rayleigh quotient]
\label{prop:rayleigh}
\index{Rayleigh quotient}
For symmetric $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} \le \dots \le \lambda_{\max}$,
$\lambda_{\min} \le \dfrac{x\T Ax}{x\T x} \le \lambda_{\max}$ for every $x \ne 0$, with equality for the corresponding eigenvectors.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $y = U\T x$, $x\T Ax/x\T x = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2/\sum_iy_i^2$, a weighted average of the eigenvalues.
\end{proof}

The Rayleigh quotient reappears in three places: minimizing $\|Ax\|^2$ over unit vectors (homogeneous least squares,
\cref{ch:numlinalg}), choosing good image features by the smallest eigenvalue of a structure tensor (\cref{ch:frontend}),
and bounding how much a matrix can amplify a vector (the matrix norm below).

\section{Trace and norms}

The trace is linear and invariant under cyclic permutations:
\begin{equation}
  \tr(AB) = \tr(BA), \qquad \tr(ABC) = \tr(CAB) = \tr(BCA),
\end{equation}
because $\tr(AB) = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}$ is symmetric in the roles of $A$ and $B$. Consequently the trace is unchanged by a
change of basis, $\tr(V\inv AV) = \tr A$, and equals the sum of the eigenvalues. A quadratic form can be written as a trace,
$x\T Ax = \tr(Axx\T)$, which is how expected values of quadratic forms are computed: $\E[x\T Ax] = \tr(A\,\E[xx\T])$
(\cref{ch:prob}).

Vector norms other than the Euclidean one are occasionally useful: $\|x\|_1 = \sum|x_i|$ and $\|x\|_\infty = \max|x_i|$. For
matrices, the \emph{Frobenius norm} $\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\tr(A\T A)}$ treats the matrix as a long vector,
and the \emph{induced 2-norm} measures the largest amplification of a vector:
\begin{equation}
  \|A\|_2 = \max_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Ax\|}{\|x\|} = \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A\T A)},
\end{equation}
the second equality being the Rayleigh quotient of $A\T A$. It equals the largest singular value of $A$ (\cref{ch:numlinalg}).
Norms are how tolerances are expressed in tests: ``$\|R\T R - I\|_F < 10^{-12}$'' is a precise statement that $R$ is
numerically orthogonal.

\begin{takeaways}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A vector is a geometric object; its coordinates depend on the chosen basis (frame). In an orthonormal basis,
        coordinates are dot products.
  \item $a\T b = \|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$; projections split a vector into parallel and perpendicular parts.
  \item $\sx{a}b = a\times b = -\sx{b}a$, $\sx{a}^3 = -\|a\|^2\sx{a}$ and $\sx{Ra} = R\sx{a}R\T$ are the workhorses of rotation algebra.
  \item A matrix is a linear map; its columns are the images of the basis vectors; products compose maps right to left.
  \item Rank--nullity: $\dim\nullsp(A) + \rank A = n$. The left nullspace is the orthogonal complement of the column space.
  \item Orthogonal matrices preserve geometry; those with determinant $+1$ are rotations. Every 3D rotation has an axis.
  \item Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors; PD matrices define ellipsoids.
\end{itemize}
\end{takeaways}

        $a \mapsto \sx{a}$ is linear and invertible.
  \item Decide whether $(1, 2, 3)$, $(4, 5, 6)$ and $(7, 8, 9)$ are independent. Find a non-trivial combination giving zero.
  \item Apply Gram--Schmidt to $(1, 1, 0)$, $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$.
  \item Prove \eqref{eq:bac-cab} by checking it for $a, b, c$ chosen among $e_1, e_2, e_3$ and using linearity.
  \item Prove \eqref{eq:skew-commutator} directly from \eqref{eq:skew-prod}, and verify the Jacobi identity
        $a\times(b\times c) + b\times(c\times a) + c\times(a\times b) = 0$ numerically.
  \item Find the matrix of the reflection of $\R^3$ across the plane orthogonal to a unit vector $n$. Show that it is
        orthogonal with determinant $-1$.
  \item Show that the eigenvalues of $\sx{a}$ are $0$ and $\pm i\|a\|$. Which eigenvector belongs to $0$?
  \item For $A = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$, find the eigen-decomposition and sketch the ellipse $x\T A\inv x = 1$.
  \item Let $H_f \in \R^{2M\times3}$ have full column rank. What is the dimension of its left nullspace for $M = 2, 3, 10$?
  \item Prove that the rank of $ab\T$ is one for non-zero $a, b$, and that every rank-one matrix has this form.
  \item Show that $\tr(A\T B)$ is an inner product on matrices, and that $\|A\|_F^2 = \tr(A\T A)$.
  \item Prove that the product of two rotation matrices is a rotation matrix, and that $R(\alpha)R(\beta) = R(\alpha + \beta)$ in the plane.
\end{exercises}
