\chapter{Preface}

\section*{What these notes are}

Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) estimates the motion of a rigid body, such as a drone, a phone, a
headset or a car, by fusing an inertial measurement unit (IMU) with a camera. It is the
navigation core of most modern augmented-reality devices and of many autonomous aerial
vehicles. VIO is also one of the best ways to learn state estimation properly, because it forces
you to deal with almost every hard topic in the field at once: nonlinear dynamics, estimation on
curved spaces, sensor noise modeling, multi-rate fusion, image processing, multi-view geometry,
unobservable directions, outliers, numerical conditioning, and real-time computation.

These notes are meant to be the only text you need. They start from vectors and matrices and end with a complete,
consistent, real-time multi-state constraint Kalman filter (MSCKF), and every tool used on the way is developed in
the notes themselves: when a chapter needs the QR decomposition, the chi-square distribution, the matrix exponential,
the inverse function theorem or image interpolation, an earlier chapter has derived it. In a university the same
material is spread over a dozen courses; the table below shows which.

The notes are written in the style of a graduate course. Each chapter states its learning
objectives, develops the theory with definitions, propositions and proofs, illustrates it with
worked examples and figures, flags the pitfalls that cost practitioners weeks of debugging, and
closes with key takeaways and exercises, followed on a new page by worked solutions to every exercise (the exercise numbers and the
solution numbers link to each other). Numerical answers in the solutions were computed, not estimated. All formulas and conventions match the \texttt{vio}
project specification in \texttt{AGENTS.md}. Every identity, Jacobian and growth law stated here has
been checked numerically. The figures and tables are generated by the Python scripts in \texttt{scripts/}
(\texttt{make\_figures.py}, \texttt{make\_tables.py} and the planar simulations in \texttt{planar\_sims.py}), with fixed random seeds,
so you can reproduce them and change their parameters.

\section*{Who should read them}

The assumed background is small:
\begin{itemize}
  \item single-variable calculus (derivatives, integrals, Taylor series) and the idea of a partial derivative;
  \item matrix arithmetic (products, transposes, solving a small linear system by hand);
  \item for the implementation notes, the ability to read C++ and Python code.
\end{itemize}
Everything else, including linear algebra beyond matrix arithmetic, probability, differential equations, Lie groups,
Kalman filtering, optimization, image processing and multi-view geometry, is developed from the beginning. Readers
who already know some of it can skip ahead; the reading paths below say where.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt, itemsep=0.4em]
  \item[Part I, Mathematical Foundations.] The vocabulary of the whole subject: linear algebra and the factorizations that
    make filters numerically sound; calculus, differential equations and their numerical solution; probability, Gaussian
    algebra and random processes; rotations, quaternions, and Lie theory, the calculus of the rotation group.
  \item[Part II, Estimation Theory.] Least squares and Bayesian estimation, nonlinear least squares and optimization on
    manifolds, linear dynamical systems, the Kalman filter and its nonlinear relatives, and the error-state formulation that
    makes estimation on a manifold clean.
  \item[Part III, Inertial Navigation.] IMU physics, noise and calibration, strapdown propagation, and the linearized error
    dynamics that drive covariance growth.
  \item[Part IV, The Error-State Filter in Practice.] Measurement updates, gating, injection and reset, and the statistics that
    tell you whether the filter is lying to you.
  \item[Part V, Computer Vision.] Camera models and calibration, image processing, corner detection and optical-flow
    tracking, epipolar geometry, robust estimation, triangulation and bundle adjustment.
  \item[Part VI, The MSCKF.] From EKF-SLAM to the multi-state constraint, stochastic cloning, the visual update with
    left-nullspace projection, a complete planar MSCKF worked by hand and in simulation, and the full algorithm.
  \item[Part VII, Beyond the Baseline.] Observability and consistency, and engineering a deterministic real-time
    implementation in C++20.
\end{description}

\section*{Reading paths}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{From scratch.} Read in order. Part I is long because it is the foundation of everything else; its
        chapters can be interleaved with the later ones as they are needed.
  \item \textbf{With an engineering mathematics background} (linear algebra, probability, ODEs). Skim
        \cref{ch:linalg,ch:numlinalg,ch:calculus,ch:prob} for notation and the results marked as theorems, read
        \cref{ch:processes,ch:rotations,ch:lie} carefully, then continue in order.
  \item \textbf{Knowing Kalman filtering.} Read \cref{ch:rotations,ch:lie,ch:eskf}, then Parts III--IV, Part V, and Parts VI--VII.
  \item \textbf{Knowing computer vision.} Read \cref{ch:rotations,ch:lie}, Parts II--IV, skim Part V, then Parts VI--VII.
  \item \textbf{Driven by the project.} Follow the milestone table below; each milestone lists the chapters it needs, and
        \cref{tab:prerequisites} tells you what to read first.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Reading the boxes}

Several kinds of callout recur throughout:

\begin{keyidea}[the one-sentence version]
The idea to remember even if you forget the derivation.
\end{keyidea}
\begin{convention}[project specification]
A rule from \texttt{AGENTS.md} that is frozen for the whole project. These are never changed
silently. A change is a logged design decision.
\end{convention}
\begin{pitfall}[what goes wrong in practice]
A mistake that is easy to make, hard to see, and expensive to debug.
\end{pitfall}
\begin{implnote}[from math to code]
How an equation becomes deterministic, allocation-free C++20 with Eigen.
\end{implnote}
\begin{projectlink}[Milestone N]
Where the material meets a project milestone and its exit criteria.
\end{projectlink}
\begin{worked}[numbers you can check by hand]
A complete calculation with concrete numbers, small enough to redo with pencil and paper.
\end{worked}
\begin{aside}[optional depth]
Background or extensions that can be skipped on a first reading.
\end{aside}

One more box, the \textbf{Derivation exercise}, marks the Jacobians that the project asks you to
derive yourself: $F_c$, $G_c$, the measurement Jacobians $H$, and the cloning Jacobian $J$. The
surrounding text teaches the method on closely related examples (the planar MSCKF of \cref{ch:planar} derives
every planar counterpart in full), and the box gives hints plus a finite-difference test that tells you when your
answer is right. The worked solutions are collected in an appendix that exists only in the \emph{solutions} edition of
these notes\ifsolutions{} (this one: \cref{app:solutions})\else{} (this edition omits it)\fi. For the same reason, the few end-of-chapter
solutions that would give away part of one of these Jacobians are replaced by a short note in the edition without solutions.

\section*{Sources and acknowledgments}

These notes stand on a body of excellent literature. The treatment of quaternions and the
error-state filter follows the conventions of Solà~\cite{sola2017quaternion}; Lie theory follows
Solà, Deray and Atchuthan~\cite{sola2018micro} and Barfoot~\cite{barfoot2017state}; the MSCKF is
due to Mourikis and Roumeliotis~\cite{mourikis2007msckf} and was refined by Li and
Mourikis~\cite{li2013msckf2}; the consistency analysis follows Huang, Mourikis and
Roumeliotis~\cite{huang2010observability}; much practical wisdom comes from OpenVINS~\cite{geneva2020openvins}
and MSCKF-VIO~\cite{sun2018msckfvio}. The annotated bibliography at the end points to the best
places to go deeper on each topic. You should never \emph{need} it to follow these notes; you may well
want it once you have.
